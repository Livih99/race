The game

Imagine you're an F1 driver racing around a track for 14 laps. Your job is to make the calls that will win the race: when to pit, how to handle trouble, and react to weather conditions.

The first 3 laps will give you a tutorial.

Lap 1: Watch the tyre health. The tyres will wear as you race.

Lap 2: Overtake. A road view shows a rival blocking one lane and green chevrons pulsing in the gap. Tap the open gap to overtake.

Lap 3: Pit. When the tyre health ring turns amber, the pit button lights up green. Tap it and pick fresh tyres.

Race briefing · 2 of 3

The rest of the race

Incidents: Something happens on the track and the race pauses so you can decide what to do. There will be 2 or 3 choices with risk pips. A countdown runs: decide fast!

Weather: When the sky darkens and you hear a radio beep, rain is coming. New tyres will appear in your menu: intermediates and wets.

Pit timing: The best stops happen when the race is calm, under a safety car, in a quiet window, so you lose the least time.

Race briefing · 3 of 3

The analogy

The car is the delivery team's systems.

The tyres wearing down is your systems getting older, higher risk and more fragile over time. Ignore it and you slow to a crawl.

The pit crew is the specialist maintenance team. Coming into the pit lane is calling them in to migrate, harden and upgrade your systems. It costs you time now but keeps you faster in the long term.

Incidents are the surprises you can't plan for, the real incidents that hit ageing systems: security vulnerabilities, instability under load, legacy components breaking down.

Reading the weather is seeing the conditions change and adapting before you are forced to.

The timer is intentional. You only get a few seconds to decide, and that pressure is real. IT faces the same pressure: incidents don't wait, windows don't stay open, and the right call has to be made fast with incomplete information. It's supposed to feel rushed. That's the point.

The lesson: the teams that win aren't the ones who pit at the last second in a panic. They're the ones who time it right, maintaining their systems before things go wrong, not after.
